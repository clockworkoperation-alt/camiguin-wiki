Overview

Camiguin is volcanic in origin, made of four stratovolcanoes, most of them with several smaller flank domes. Hibok-Hibok is the only one with recorded historical eruptions, the last in 1953.[1]

How many volcanoes? Counts vary with what is counted. Wikipedia lists four main volcanoes plus many domes,[1] while a GMA travel guide notes that the island has more volcanoes than its five municipalities.[9] Popular sources often quote seven.

Birth of Mount Vulcan

Earthquakes began on 16 February 1871 and grew stronger until 30 April, when a fissure opened about 370 m southwest of the village of Catarman. Mount Vulcan grew from that vent and kept pouring lava into the sea.[1]

When the Challenger expedition passed in 1875, it described a dome about 590 m high, still smoking and glowing at the top. Old Catarman was destroyed and part of it sank below sea level, including its cemetery. A large cross was raised over the Sunken Cemetery in 1982.[1]

  1. Feb 1871

    Earthquakes start on 16 February and intensify.[1]

  2. Apr 1871

    A fissure opens near Catarman on 30 April and Mount Vulcan begins to grow.[1]

  3. 1875

    The Challenger expedition records a smoking dome around 590 m tall.[1]

  4. 1982

    The cross marking the Sunken Cemetery is erected.[1]

Hibok-Hibok, 1948–1953

After earthquake swarms in late August 1948, Hibok-Hibok began a five-year eruptive period.[7] The worst came on the morning of 4 December 1951, when lava, poisonous gases and pyroclastic flows destroyed nearly 19 km² of land, mostly in Mambajao.[1]

  1. 1948

    An explosive eruption inside the summit crater on 1 September opens the eruptive period.[7]

  2. 1949

    An eruption kills 79 people through landslides.[2]

  3. 1950

    On 15 September, another explosive eruption devastates the northern slope and starts the Ilihan lava flow.[7]

  4. 1951

    The largest eruption strikes on 4 December, three weeks before Christmas, and triggers an evacuation that halves the population.[3]

  5. 1953

    The last recorded eruption, on 14 July, produces small ash emissions.[7]

Disputed death toll. Sources disagree on how many died in 1951. PHIVOLCS material cited by MindaNews gives more than 3,000 deaths,[5] a 2023 provincial report via the Philippine Information Agency gives more than 500,[6] and the UP Resilience Institute says hundreds.[7]

Legacy and monitoring

The 1951 disaster led the government to create the Commission on Volcanology in June 1952. It later became the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology (PHIVOLCS).[10]

PHIVOLCS runs an observatory in Sitio Quiboro, about 4.7 km from the crater, with seismic sensors in Mainit (Catarman), on the peak of Mount Vulcan, and on the upper slope of Hibok-Hibok in Tagdo, Mambajao.[10]

On 22 July 2026, for the eruption's 75th anniversary, PHIVOLCS and the provincial government unveiled a commemorative marker at the Mambajao Municipal Hall.[4] The volcano was then at Alert Level 0, meaning no eruption is expected soon, but it remains active and monitored.[3]

Protected area

In 2004, the island's forested core, including Mount Timpoong and Hibok-Hibok, became the Timpoong and Hibok-Hibok Natural Monument. In 2015 it was declared an ASEAN Heritage Park. It is the island's last remaining forest and home to most of its endemic wildlife.[1]

Seventy-five years after 1951, Hibok-Hibok is quiet, but still watched.
